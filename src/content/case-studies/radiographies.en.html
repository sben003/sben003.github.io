01

One class in four

Reading a chest X-ray takes time and depends on the specialist's eye. The question is binary: normal or pneumonia. The working set holds 5,216 labelled X-rays — and it is heavily imbalanced.

This imbalance decides how to evaluate. Accuracy alone flatters the majority class; one has to look, class by class, at precision (when the model says “pneumonia”, is it right?) and recall (of the real pneumonia cases, how many does it find?). The confusion matrix, more than the overall percentage, tells whether a model is usable.

02

Two ways of looking at an image

A Hand-crafted descriptors

  1. Greyscale X-ray
  2. Gabor · PHOG · DCT · Fourier
  3. Z-score normalisation
  4. Logistic regression
  5. 96.5% on test

B Convolutional network (CNN)

  1. 150 × 150 X-ray
  2. 3 convolutions · 32, 64, 128 filters
  3. Dense 512 + dropout 0.5
  4. Sigmoid output
  5. 87.5% on validation

A — Telling the model what to look at

The first approach breaks each X-ray down into measures chosen in advance, capturing textures, edges and frequencies. Two families complement each other: global filters, which move the whole image into the frequency domain, and local filters, which scan small regions for edges and orientations.

The four descriptors extracted from each X-ray.
DescriptorScopeWhat it captures
Fourier transformGlobalThe image's spatial frequencies: periodic patterns, fine textures.
DCTGlobalIntensity variations by frequency, concentrated in the low frequencies — the principle behind JPEG compression.
Gabor filtersLocalTextures and edges in a given orientation; four orientations tested (0, π/4, π/2, 3π/4).
PHOGLocalHistograms of oriented gradients over a pyramid of cells: the shape of contours at several scales.

These descriptors do not share a scale: Gabor responses between 0 and 255 sit next to Fourier coefficients close to zero. Without Z-score normalisation — centring, then scaling each variable — the optimiser would favour the large values. The normalised vectors then feed a logistic regression.

B — Letting the network learn its filters

The CNN takes the raw image and learns its own convolution filters. Three convolutional layers (32, 64 then 128 filters of 3 × 3, each followed by max-pooling), a dense layer of 512 neurons, 50% dropout and a sigmoid output, trained with Adam on a binary cross-entropy loss.

The model summary reveals an internal imbalance: of its 19,034,177 parameters, 18,940,416 — 99.5% — sit in the single dense layer after flattening. A model that size, fed a few thousand images, has all the capacity it needs to learn them by heart.

03

From 73 X-rays to 5,216

three lab sessions, one final report

Lab 01Explore

Load the X-rays, convert them to greyscale, resize them, flatten them into vectors. Apply the four transforms one by one to see what each brings out of the same image.

Lab 02Extract

PHOG coded by hand — gradients, orientations, histograms over three pyramid levels —, one CSV file per descriptor. In parallel, a first CNN in PyTorch: 56% training accuracy at the first epoch, 94.5% at the fourth, then 89% at the fifth. A first sign of overfitting.

Lab 03Structure

From CSV to HDF5 (h5py) to store vectors and labels compactly. CNN rewritten in Keras with a third convolution and dropout. On a sample of 73 X-rays, 66.7% on test: far too few images to conclude anything.

FinalCompare

Full set of 5,216 X-rays. Three variants evaluated: descriptors concatenated into a single vector, descriptors kept in separate columns, and the CNN. Confusion matrix, precision, recall and F1 for the descriptors; accuracy and loss curves for the CNN.

Keeping the descriptors in separate columns rather than a single vector changes almost nothing: 272 and 734 correct answers instead of 273 and 733, the same 96.5% accuracy. The storage layout is not what drives the result; the point of separate columns lies elsewhere — being able to measure each descriptor's contribution afterwards.

The convolutional network

The first training runs over 10 epochs reached 100% training accuracy: the network was learning the images by heart. The number of epochs was reduced, and the validation set, only 16 images to begin with, raised to 67. Even so, the validation curve stays erratic.

Going from 90.6% to 33.3% then to 100% from one epoch to the next does not describe a model that is improving: with 67 images, a handful of X-rays is enough to swing the score. This validation set is too small to judge the CNN — the main lesson of this part.

The simplest model wins

The final choice went to the descriptors and logistic regression: reliable on an imbalanced set, simple to interpret and tune, and the best measured figures. The CNN is not ruled out: it remains the natural route with more images, data augmentation and class rebalancing.

That is the lesson I keep from this project: a more powerful model is not a better model until you have the data — and the evaluation protocol — to prove it.

06

What I would do differently today

01Normalise after splitting

In the code, the Z-score normalisation is fitted on all the X-rays before the train / test split: the mean and standard deviation have seen the test data. The effect is probably small here, but it is information leakage. A scikit-learn Pipeline rules it out by design.

02Compare on the same ground

The regression is evaluated on 1,043 test X-rays, the CNN on 67 validation X-rays: the two figures do not really compare. I would set aside a common test set from the start, and use stratified cross-validation for tuning.

03Pick the threshold by cost

20 missed pneumonia cases against 17 false alarms: in a medical setting, the costly error is the first one. I would set the decision threshold to guarantee a target recall on the pneumonia class, and report that recall before accuracy.

04Give the CNN a fair chance

Data augmentation, class weights, early stopping on a reasonably sized validation set, and a dense head lighter than 18.9 million parameters. The routes the report had already identified, but had no time to follow.
