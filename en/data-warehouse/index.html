01

The question being asked

The food industry long relied on synthetic colourants, regularly criticised for their potential health effects and their environmental footprint. The trend is shifting towards natural colourants — but that shift is hard to reason about without data: does a natural colourant sell as well? Is its health profile genuinely better? Which origins are the most sustainable?

Answering means crossing quantities that do not live in the same table: commercial measures (volumes, revenue) and qualitative scores (health impact, environmental impact), read along several axes — the product, the colourant, its origin, its supplier, the period.

That is precisely the problem dimensional modelling solves. Hence the starting point: identify the facts to measure, then the dimensions that give them meaning.

The four measures retained

  • Quantity (kg) — mass of colourant moved on the sale.
  • Sale amount (€) — revenue generated.
  • Health score (%) — estimated health impact index.
  • Environmental score (%) — environmental impact index.

The first two belong to commercial performance, the last two to product responsibility. Making them coexist in one fact table is what makes the cross-analysis possible.

02

A star schema, not just another table

Kimball method

The design rule is simple, and everything else rests on it: the fact table holds only foreign keys and measures; each dimension describes one reference entity only, with unique values. No redundancy, no descriptive attribute inside the facts.

That split is not theoretical: it proved decisive twice. First in Talend, where joins stay legible instead of getting lost in one wide file. Then in Power BI, where aggregations follow each axis naturally without having to replay joins on every visual.

Fact table metadata. Every measure is typed and bounded before any insert.
AttributeTypeRole
id_venteINTPrimary key
id_temps, id_produit, id_colorant, id_origine, id_fournisseurINTForeign keys to the five dimensions
quantite_kgFLOATMeasure — mass sold
montant_venteFLOATMeasure — revenue, computed during ETL
score_santeFLOATMeasure — percentage bounded 0–100
score_envFLOATMeasure — percentage bounded 0–100

Volumes were calibrated deliberately: 100 sales in the fact table, with more compact dimensions of 30 to 65 rows. That ratio reproduces what production looks like — many facts pointing at few reference entities — while keeping enough variety for cross-analysis to mean something.

03

The ETL chain in Talend

The EDD_Colorants job, built in Talend Open Studio, reads a single source file, transforms it and loads it into the warehouse. Consolidating everything into one file — Colorants_Entrepot_Complet.csv — is worth explaining: it allows a single read component, transformations in one flow, and Power BI preparation without extra joins.

What happens before Talend

No file enters the flow unchecked. With my project partner we systematically verified three things: typing (prices, quantities, dates, scores), the absence of duplicates in the dimensions, and the correspondence of primary and foreign keys. An ETL that starts on inconsistent data does not raise an error: it produces a wrong result, which is far worse.

The Talend components in the job, and what each one does in the flow.
ComponentStageRole
tFileInputDelimited Extract Reads the CSV row by row — comma separator, UTF-8 encoding, dates in the dd/MM/yyyy pattern
tMap Transform Joins between sources, computed fields, derivations, filtering out unused columns
tDBOutput (MySQL) Load Writes dimensions and fact table into the warehouse, table recreated on every run
tLogRow Check Prints the outgoing flow so what transits can be verified by eye

The heart of the flow: tMap

This is where raw data becomes workable. Two transformations are worth citing because they illustrate warehouse logic well.

The sale amount is not read, it is computed: montant_vente = quantite_kg × prix_unitaire, the price coming from the colourant dimension. Storing an already-computed amount in the source would have allowed two columns to disagree; deriving it during ETL guarantees it always matches its components.

The quarter is derived from the month rather than entered. The time dimension is rebuilt from the sale date — day, month, quarter, year — which gives the analysis a full hierarchical granularity without asking anything of the source.

An operational detail The load is configured as "drop the table if it exists and create it". The job is therefore replayable at will: every run rebuilds the warehouse from the source, with no accumulation and no duplicates. On a batch-fed warehouse, that property beats a fragile incremental load.

04

The warehouse

The target database, EntrepotDeDonnees, is a MySQL database hosted on a local XAMPP environment. The choice is deliberately modest and it is defensible: what is being demonstrated here is not the ability to absorb volume, but the correctness of the model and the cleanliness of the chain that feeds it.

Each dimension is built by its own flow, and the fact table is assembled last — it cannot exist until its five dimensions are populated, since it contains nothing but references to them. That load order is not a detail: it is what guarantees the warehouse's referential integrity.

An intermediate database served as a staging area between extraction and transformation, to avoid re-reading the source file on every change to the flow.

05

The OLAP cube

Pentaho Schema Workbench

A warehouse answers queries; a cube answers questions. Modelled in Pentaho Schema Workbench, it exposes the fact table and its dimensions in navigable form, with analysis hierarchies — Year > Month > Day for time — that let you drill down or roll up without rewriting a query.

On top of the cube we defined eight analytical data marts: as many thematic subsets, each answering one precise question. That is the concrete payoff of a dimensional model — one structure, eight angles of reading.

06

Power BI reporting

Power BI connects directly to the EntrepotDeDonnees database and imports the model's tables. Because the schema is a star, the relationships almost infer themselves and aggregations follow each axis without extra handling.

What the visuals show

  • Grouped bars — sales volumes set against health scores, split by year and by product category.
  • Stacked bars — the distribution of health scores by source of origin and by year, which brings out the weight of plant-based origins.
  • Pie and donut — the split of sales and quality scores by colourant, putting market share and qualitative performance side by side.
  • Time series — distribution by source and quantity sold per quarter, then the evolution of health scores by colourant nature.

On the simulated dataset, the cross-reading surfaces an interesting tension: synthetic colourants hold the majority market share, while natural ones, less present by volume, post markedly higher quality scores and greater stability between commercial performance and health profile.

What this is worth, and what it is not These observations describe a dataset we built ourselves. They confirm that the chain faithfully returns what was put into it and that the analysis axes work — not that they constitute a finding about the real food colourant market.

07

Review

The project covers a full business-intelligence chain: needs analysis, dimensional modelling, ETL, warehousing, OLAP cube, reporting. Four tools follow on from one another — Talend Open Studio, MySQL, Pentaho Schema Workbench, Power BI — and every handover imposes a contract: a file format, a relational schema, a connection, a data model.

What I take from it

The most lasting lesson is not in the tooling but in the discipline of modelling. A badly split star schema does not show immediately: it is paid for later, in unreadable ETL joins and wrong aggregations in the reporting. Deciding upfront what is a fact and what is a dimension governs everything downstream.

That method carried over afterwards, in an entirely different context, to the marine environmental monitoring platform I built at the end of my M1 — where the data model question, this time a time series, was again the structuring decision.

The limits

The dataset is simulated and the volumes modest: nothing here demonstrates behaviour at scale. The load is full on every run, which suits this volume but would not hold on a continuously fed warehouse — that would call for an incremental load and slowly changing dimension handling.
